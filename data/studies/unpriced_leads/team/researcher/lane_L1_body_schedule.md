# L1 literature lane: body, schedule and conditions

I accessed every URL on 13 Oct 2026. The read level is given per source. "Tool summary" means I read the page through the fetch tool's summary, not word for word. My web-search allowance (200 calls, shared with the other agents in this turn) ran out near the end, so the gaps are listed at the bottom.

## Summary

- **The one direct market test in this lane says the market prices it.** Burke et al. (NBER 2023; I read the full text) find that a player whose previous match was hotter than the opponent's wins less often, by about −0.1% per °C. Bookmakers, Pinnacle included, already price this on 2002–17 data, and betting on it loses money. This lowers the prior for simple heat features. Interactions such as heat × minutes or heat × age were not tested.
- **No peer-reviewed tennis study tests fatigue or turnaround against odds.** Sackmann's newer posts compare against rating models only. A title "hangover" is essentially null (all finalists about 1% worse than expected), and extra rest from a walkover is worth nothing (48.2% won vs 48.9% predicted). The only market evidence of schedule effects comes from other sports: NBA back-to-backs plus eastward travel against the spread (2010), and NFL circadian effects against the spread (2013). Both are old enough that those markets may have learned them since.
- **Travel in tennis shows nothing.** The one tennis study, Maynard 2026 (already known to the project), finds no link between travel and match results. Effects in other sports appear only after eastward travel and are disputed. This agrees with the project's finding that travel is priced. Not a priority.
- **The newest strong evidence comes from injury research, and none of it has been tested against results or odds.** Kovalchik (2020) estimates the causal effect of cumulative match load on time lost to injury (HR 1.05 per 1,000 extra games, growing with age). A 2025 review names spikes in recent load relative to a player's usual load (the acute:chronic ratio) as the strongest outside predictor of injury. Retirements are rising: a record 4.8% of ATP matches in 2025. One group reports that men's retirement rates double every five years after age 27. All of this extends the "schedule and body" family the close misses.
- **Conditions changed in 2025–26:**
  - the ATP heat rule (2026);
  - the late-finish rules (2025; post-midnight finishes fell from 50 to 39);
  - ball centralisation (2025–27);
  - large swings in measured court pace at the same venue (Paris 2025: CPI 35.1, down from about 46; Shanghai: 32.9, down from 40.8).

  None has been tested against the market.
- **Age curves are well described, but no study tests age against odds.** Two untested interactions point in opposite directions: in heat, older players do relatively better (Simani 2026); under load, older players get injured more (Kovalchik 2020). Either could help explain why age survives against the close.
- **The circadian evidence is too thin to stand alone.** The best is a lab study of 12 state-level players: serve speed 10.5 km/h lower at 8 pm than at 2 pm.

## Candidate statistics (not in the model)

1. **Load spike and season load × age** (highest priority; all from data the project already has).
   - Definition: an acute:chronic ratio = games played in the last 14 days ÷ the average 14-day games total over the previous 56 days. Also games played in the last 52 weeks × max(0, age − 27).
   - Mechanism: the close sees match counts, but probably not load relative to a player's own normal, or how age amplifies load. The injury cost shows up as flat performances and retirements, and the market reacts mostly to visible injury news.
   - Evidence: Kovalchik 2020; the 2025 review; the retirement doubling after 27.
   - Difference from the model: it has sets in the last 14 days (an absolute count) and age, not this ratio or the interaction.
   - Caveat: the effect partly runs through retirements, so it depends on how the venue settles retired matches.
2. **Cause-specific rust ("injury signal").**
   - Definition: a flag for a retirement or walkover by the player in the last 30 days; matches played since a layoff of 8 weeks or more (1, 2–3, 4–10); and entry on a protected ranking.
   - Mechanism: "days since last match on this surface" mixes surface switches with injuries. Rust passed discovery on 2014–24 but is unresolved on 2025–26; splitting it by cause may sharpen it.
   - Evidence: Sackmann 2018 (known); George 2019; Casals 2025 (injury is the main known cause of walkovers).
   - Market: no test found. The direction (over- or under-reaction) is unknown.
3. **Turnaround hours and late finishes.**
   - Definition: hours between the end of the previous match and the scheduled start of this one; flags for under 20 hours, a finish after 23:00 local time, and two matches in one day.
   - Mechanism: books and sharp models likely use days and sets, not clock time.
   - Evidence: indirect only — the Turner lab studies, the NBA and NFL spread results, and Sackmann's time on court (known).
   - Power: low, with only about 39–50 post-midnight finishes per ATP season, and the 2025 rules change the regime. Pool it with short turnarounds at Challengers.
   - Data: needs real start and finish times (Burke scraped start times from a betting site).
4. **Minutes on court in the last 72 hours, relative to the player's normal match length.**
   - This differs from "sets in 14 days" in timing and intensity: long sets, tiebreaks and deuce games count.
   - It is likely partly priced. Test it as a complement within the same cluster as candidates 1 and 3.
5. **Heat-weighted recent load, and heat × age.**
   - Definition: minutes × max(0, WBGT − 25°C) over each player's matches in the last 3 days, minus the opponent's; plus forecast WBGT at the match × the age difference.
   - Prior: lowered by Burke (simple lagged heat is priced). The untested parts are the interaction with minutes, the effect of the 2026 cooling break, and Challenger/ITF events.
   - Data: hourly venue weather plus start times; outdoor matches only.
6. **Court-pace change at this edition** (cross-player, so it must use the leak-proof clock).
   - Definition: this edition's pace so far (aces and holds against players' expected rates, from matches surely finished by `lf + 1`) minus the event's previous edition, × the difference in the two players' serve/return profiles.
   - Mechanism: early rounds are priced on the venue's reputation, and the model's profiles are per surface with long memory, so it cannot see changes like Paris and Shanghai in 2025.
   - Evidence: the CPI figures (secondhand), the ITF serve-return physics, and Sackmann's ace factors.
   - Market: no test found.
7. **Ball switch** (low priority).
   - Definition: the ball model differs from the player's previous event, and the number of different balls used in the last 4 weeks.
   - The only evidence is player complaints, and centralisation removes most of the variation by 2027. Not recommended unless the data are cheap.

Not recommended: time-zone and eastward-travel features. Tennis results show nothing, the project's own check found travel priced, and the evidence from other sports is disputed.
