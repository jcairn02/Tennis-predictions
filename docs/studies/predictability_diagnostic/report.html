Summary

What we found

Can information known before a tennis match predict the winner better than an Elo rating, and better than the bookmakers' closing prices? We tested 59 candidate features (325 versions in all) on 155,130 men's and 60,321 women's professional matches from 2013 to 2026, always predicting each season from earlier seasons only.

observedThe bookmakers stay ahead

No feature improves on their closing prices by more than 0.3% of the way from a coin flip to their prices: far too little to bet on.

observedA few things beat Elo, modestly

Serve-and-return strength ratings do best: they close about 28% (men) and 29% (women) of the gap between Elo and the bookmakers. Recent form, recent workload and a new surface add less.

inferredPre-match records look priced in

If there is an edge, it is more likely in live (in-play) data, or in markets less sharp than the bookmakers' closing prices.

What we suggest next

  • suggestion Test the best features together. One model with the strength ratings, recent points won and recent workload, checked against the bookmakers. The repaired feature comparison supersedes the original results. Failure of these features would not close the pre-match research route.
  • suggestion Try live (in-play) data. Point observations may help update day-level performance estimates. Testing this needs time-stamped point data and live prices, which this study did not have.

What the table holds

ObservedTwo tables. stage0_matches has one row per match: its level, round, surface, indoor or outdoor where known, best of three or five sets, tournament and date, status, score, length in minutes, which source it came from, and a place in one chronological order. stage0_match_players has one row per player per match: side A or B, won or lost, ranking, ranking points, seed, entry route, age, height, hand, country, the nine box-score counts, and each rate below stored as its numerator and denominator. A match's side A is set by a hash of its id, never by who won, their ranking or their name, so side A won 50.0% of all matches.

ObservedSources. Jeff Sackmann's files are the base: 461,670 ATP and 405,621 WTA matches. Tennis My Life (TML), a site that rebuilds the same format from official results, adds the matches Sackmann lacks: 3,951 ATP and 912 WTA matches from the weeks after Sackmann's files stop (25 May 2026 for the main tours, 1–2 June for the lower levels), and 494 ATP and 56 older ones. Of TML's 196,332 rows, 190,877 were matched to a Sackmann match first, by the two players' names, then by one player in the same tournament and round, then by player ids. A match is listed once.

Level
How big the event is. Grand Slams (the four majors), the Tour Finals (season finales), then the 1000s, 500s and 250s of the ATP and WTA tours (the number is the ranking points the winner gets), then the Challenger / WTA 125 tier below them, then the ITF tier at the bottom. Team events are the Davis Cup, Billie Jean King Cup, ATP Cup, United Cup, Laver Cup and the Olympics.
Qualifying
A small knockout event just before the main draw; its winners take the last places in the main draw.
Round
Q1–Q3 are qualifying rounds; R128 to F are the main draw (round of 128 down to the final). RR is a round robin, where each player of a group meets the others.
Box score
Nine counts per player per match: aces, double faults, points served, first serves in, points won on the first serve, points won on the second serve, service games, and break points saved and faced (a break point is a point that would win the game for the receiver).
Retirement, walkover
A player who stops during a match has retired; the opponent advances and is recorded as the winner. A walkover is a withdrawal before the match starts: no point is played.
Tiebreak
A set that reaches 6-6 is decided by a first-to-7-points tiebreak. A match tiebreak (first to 10 points) replaces the deciding set in some formats.
Date
Every date is the Monday of the event's week, not the day the match was played. A week's events are therefore simultaneous in the order; rounds give the order within an event.

Stage 1

How predictable is tennis?

Stage 1 asks how far the simplest predictors get. Each gives, before a match, a probability that one player wins: a coin, the players' rankings, an Elo rating built from every earlier result, and the bookmakers' closing odds. Each is scored on the 155,130 men's and 60,321 women's evaluation matches of 2013 to September 2026 by log loss: the average, over matches, of minus the natural log of the probability given to the player who won. A coin, always 50%, scores 0.693; lower is better, and a thousandth (0.001) is one milli-log-loss. The odds exist only for tour-level main draws, so every comparison with the market is made on the same matches. The headline window is 2022–2026.

The baselines, in plain words

Walk-forward
Every probability is made before the match, from earlier matches only (earlier weeks, and earlier rounds of the same event). Nothing is fitted on the match it predicts.
Ranking
The ATP and WTA rankings order players by the points they earned over the last 52 weeks; ranking points are those points. Here, a one-input logistic regression turns the log of the ratio of the two ranks (and, separately, of the two players' points) into a probability, refitted each season on the seasons before it. A player with no ranking is given the rank just below the lowest seen on the tour in earlier seasons, and no points.
Elo
A rating that rises when a player wins and falls when they lose, by more when the result was a surprise; the gap between two ratings gives the probability. It is run over every singles match since 2010, down to the ITF level, so newcomers arrive with a rating. A player's rating moves fast over their first matches and settles as they play more (the K factor shrinks with the number of matches).
Surface Elo
Each player also has a separate rating on each surface (hard, clay, grass, carpet), moved only by matches on it. The prediction mixes the two: 70% overall and 30% surface for the men, 75% and 25% for the women.
Closing odds
The last bookmaker prices before a match, as tennis-data.co.uk records them (its own words: "generally the most recent before play starts"): the average over bookmakers of each player's decimal odds. Decimal odds of 2.50 return 2.50 for each 1 staked, so 1/2.50 = 40% is the implied probability. The two players' implied probabilities add to a little more than one (the bookmakers' margin, or overround: median 1.058), so they are scaled to add to one.
Favourite
The player a baseline gives more than 50%.
Calibration
Whether favourites given 70% win about 70% of the time.
Tuning years
Elo's few constants were chosen on 2013–2018 and then frozen, so later seasons are a fair test.

How far each baseline gets

Anatomy of a win

Stage 2 looks inside matches: what decides who wins, read from each match's own statistics, its box score (points won on serve and on return, aces, double faults, break points) and its score. None of this is a pre-match forecast: every number uses the match's own events, so it says how matches are won, not how to predict them. It covers 146,971 men's and 33,205 women's evaluation matches that were completed and have a box score (the women's from 2019, as the Brief sets for box scores). Retirements are set aside: their statistics cover part of a match that something else decided.

One device runs through the section, the pure-luck replay. Every match is played again, point by point, as if each point were an independent draw: the server wins it with a fixed probability, whatever the score, at the serve rates the two players showed that day. The replay has no skill on big points, no nerves and no streaks. What the real matches show beyond the replay is something more than scoring luck; what they share with it is not.

Terms used in this section

Points share
The share of all the points in a match that a player won, on serve and on return together. The points edge is that share minus 50%.
Serve rate
The share of the points on their own serve that a player won in the match. Serving wins most points: on average 60.3–66.3% for the men and 54.5–58.5% for the women, by surface.
Break point
A point that would give the receiver the server's game (a break) if the receiver won it. The server saves it by winning it; the receiver converts it.
Tiebreak
At six games all a set is decided by a race to 7 points, won by two. Grand Slam deciding sets have their own rules (a race to 10, a tiebreak only at 12 games all, or none), listed in stage2_slam_final_set_rules.csv.
Best of three, five
The first to win two sets, or three. Only men's Grand Slam main draws (and, in most years, Wimbledon's last qualifying round) are best of five. The deciding set is the one played at one set all, or two all.
The iid model
Every point an independent draw ("independent and identically distributed"): the server wins it with their serve rate, whatever the score. From the two serve rates it gives the exact chance of winning a game, a tiebreak, a set and the match, under the match's format.
Expected
What the iid model says a player should have won: break points saved = break points faced times the serve rate; tiebreaks = the model's chance of winning a tiebreak from the two serve rates. Over-performance is the actual count minus this.
Plug-in floor
The iid model's chance that a player wins, fed the serve rates measured in the match itself.
Pure-luck replay
The same matches played again point by point under the iid model. The measured rates carry noise (a rate measured over one match's serve points can sit several points from the player's true rate that day), so the replay draws each player's true rate from what the measured one allows, shrinking it toward the tour's average for that surface by the amount the noise implies.
Log loss
As in Stage 1: the average of minus the natural log of the probability given to the player who won; a coin scores 0.693, lower is better, and 0.001 is one milli-log-loss.
Split-half correlation
Each player's matches are split into two random halves; the correlation, across players, between a measure in one half and in the other. A stable trait correlates highly; luck does not correlate.

2. Beyond the points: break points and tiebreaks

Given the points share, how much do break points and tiebreaks explain? A logistic regression of the result on the points share alone scores a log loss of 0.117 (men) and 0.118 (women), five-fold cross-validated. Two inputs are then added: break points won above expectation (saves on serve and conversions on return together, in break points), and tiebreaks won above expectation.

ObservedBreak points add 2.9 milli-log-loss for the men (95% range 2.5 to 3.3) and 0.2 for the women (-0.0 to +0.5); tiebreaks add 40.9 and 24.9; both, 45.5 and 25.6. In the pure-luck replay the same steps give 3.0, 0.4; 46.6, 29.9; and 51.6, 31.0.

InferredTiebreaks, not break points, carry what the points share misses: a tiebreak settles a whole set on a dozen points. They do so about as much as pure luck predicts, slightly less (the real matches play fewer tiebreaks than the replay: 0.379 a match against 0.427 for the men).

ObservedMeasured against their serve rate in the match, servers seem to save more break points than expected: 59.25% against 58.41% for the men. That is an artefact of the measure: in the replay, where points are pure luck, it gives 59.77% against 58.19%. A player who faces many break points has usually had an unlucky day on serve, which drags the rate they are measured against below their true one (the tests show the bias is absent against the true rate). InferredAgainst the replay, real servers save 0.7 points fewer break points than pure luck gives for the men and 0.6 fewer for the women: a small pressure effect.

ObservedWinners won 80.2% of the tiebreaks they played, against 59.7% their serve rates predict (men; replay 79.1% against 59.8%): winning tiebreaks is much of how matches are won, and selection, not skill, makes winners look clutch. Of the matches won with fewer points, the winner was ahead of expectation on break points or tiebreaks in 87.6% (men) and 77.4% (women); in the replay 90.2% and 80.3%.

3. The randomness floor

For each match, the iid model gives the chance that a player wins from the two players' serve rates in that match, with the match's format and its deciding-set rule. That is the plug-in floor: roughly the best anyone could do knowing how each player would play that day. ObservedOn the 2022–2026 matches with closing odds it scores 0.237 for the men and 0.194 for the women, against 0.585 and 0.585 for the market and 0.607 and 0.604 for surface Elo. Taken at face value it splits a coin's uncertainty into 107.7 milli-log-loss the market knew before the match, 348.6 for how the players played on the day, and 236.8 of scoring luck (men).

InferredThe plug-in floor is optimistic, more than "rough" allows. The rates are measured on the very points that decided the match, so a player who won the match by luck also shows a higher rate; as a forecast of the real result the plug-in chances are far too timid, and refitting them needs their log-odds multiplied by 2.23 (men) and 2.44 (women). The pure-luck replay shows the same: its plug-in chances need 2.23 and 2.76, and its own plug-in floor, 0.226 and 0.197, is close to the real one. In the replay the true rates are known: a forecaster who knew them would score 0.426 and 0.421. That is the better floor. With it, of a coin's 693 milli-log-loss the closing odds remove 107.7 (16%), knowing how each player would play that day would remove 159.9 more (23%), and 425.5 (61%) is scoring luck no forecast can remove (men; women 16%, 24% and 61%).

ObservedLonger matches hold less luck: on the men's matches with odds of 2013–2026, the corrected floor is 0.359 at best of five against 0.438 at best of three, and the market 0.488 against 0.596.

5. Which points make the edge

A player wins points on serve and on return. But a player's return points against the first serve are the opponent's first-serve points lost, so in a match serving well and returning well are one contest seen from either side, and the parts below are the two players' difference on each kind of point. The points edge (share minus 50%) splits exactly into six parts: aces; first serves in play (points that start with a first serve in that is not an ace); first serves in (landing more first serves, which win more often than second serves); second serves in play; double faults; and serve-point count (who served more points).

ObservedMen's winners take 5.6 points more per 100 played than an even share. Of these, 3.1 come from first serves in play, 2.0 from second serves in play, 0.4 from aces, 0.2 from double faults and 0.2 from first serves in; the serve-point count takes 0.3 away, as a winner's service games are shorter. Across matches, first serves in play account for 55% of the variation in the points edge, second serves in play 37%, aces 7% (grass 10%, clay 5%), double faults 5% and first serves in 3% (women: 60%, 32%, 5%, 4% and 2%).

InferredThe edge is made in the exchange once the serve is in play, far more than by aces, double faults or the first-serve percentage. A regression of the points share on the six differences explains 97.6% of its variation and apportions it alike (the table). 7. Big points, point by point (Grand Slams, the optional part)

Jeff Sackmann's Grand Slam point-by-point files record who served and who won every point of most main-draw singles matches of 2011–2024. Each match was replayed point by point and checked against the file (the set, the games where written, the score in every game, and who serves): 4,985 of 5,336 men's and 4,963 of 5,172 women's matches pass (1,824,960 points); most of the rest end before the match does (retirements, or files cut short). Each point gets an importance: how much winning it rather than losing it raises the chance of winning the match, from the score alone, with both players at their tour's average serve rate in these files (63.9% men, 57.0% women). Big-point play ("clutch") is a player's importance-weighted share of points won minus their plain share, on serve and on return, in the same match. Every result is set beside pure-luck replays of the same matches, point by point.

ObservedImportance is concentrated: the most important tenth of points carries 31% of all importance (men) and 26% (women). Break points are 8.3% of men's points and 17.8% of the importance, 2.1 times an average point; tiebreak points 2.8 times.

ObservedOn serve, players win slightly fewer of the big points than of their serve points overall: -0.6 points (men) and -0.3 (women). Pure luck gives -0.3 and -0.2, so the real shortfall beyond luck is -0.3 and -0.2: the same small pressure effect as the break points. Adding the big-point difference to the points share improves the log loss by 102.0 milli-log-loss for the men and 91.7 for the women; pure luck gives 117.3 and 88.9. As with tiebreaks, big points decide close matches, about as much as luck predicts.

InferredDoes big-point play carry over? On serve, no more than luck. On return it does: with at least 10 matches in each half, 0.21 for the men (95% range 0.04 to 0.37) and 0.19 for the women (0.02 to 0.35), against 0.01 and -0.01 in the replays. This is the one place in Stage 2 where play on big points looks like more than luck. It rests on 131 men and 134 women with many Grand Slam matches, and it could also come from form that swings within matches, which the replays do not model. Pure luck itself makes big-point play look persistent for players with many matches, because they win most of them; that is why the replays, not zero, are the benchmark.

Terms used in this section

Rate
A per-match statistic as a share: serve points won of serve points played, aces per serve point, break points saved of break points faced, and so on. Its units are what it is counted in (serve points, break points faced, tiebreaks, sets, games).
Reliability
Of a rate measured on a sample of matches: the share of its spread across players that is real difference between players, not chance. 1 is all player, 0 all chance.
Split-half correlation
Two separate random sets of n matches from each player's season; the rate on each, correlated across players. It equals the reliability of a sample of n matches.
Spearman–Brown curve
How reliability grows with the sample: r = n / (n + k). k is the sample at which reliability reaches 0.5, half signal and half noise. Twice the sample, two-thirds signal; four times, four-fifths.
Regular, typical season
A regular plays at least 20 evaluation-level matches in a season. A typical season is the median regular's: 44 matches for the men and 37 for the women over all surfaces (on one surface, the median of regulars who played on it).
Shrinkage
Mixing the tour's average into a player's rate in proportion to how little the player's own sample says: (x + K × average) / (d + K), with x the player's count and d their units. K, the shrinkage constant, is chance variation per unit over the true spread between players; a player with d = K units gets half their own rate and half the average.
Carry-over
The correlation of a player's shrunk rate in one season with the next, or on one surface with another in the same season. The ceiling is what it would be if the true rate never changed, given the samples' noise.
Serve and return ratings
Each player's serve and return strength on the log-odds scale. The chance that a server wins a serve point is 1 / (1 + e−(μ + serve − return)), with μ the tour's average on the surface. They are updated match by match on the serve points each player won.
Opponent-adjusted rate
A match's serve (or return) points won restated as against a typical returner (server), using the opponent's rating before the match.
Log loss per point
As Stage 1's log loss, but scored on every point (or game, tiebreak...) of the next match with the forecast rate; milli-log-loss per point better than the tour's average for the surface.

2. Reliability against sample size

For each n, players with at least 2n matches in a window (a season; three seasons on grass, where a season is thin) give two separate random sets of n matches; the rate on each is correlated across players, averaged over 20 random draws. The Spearman–Brown curve is fitted to the n's with at least 300 player-windows, in matches and in the rate's units. ObservedServe points won reach half signal after 8.3 matches for the men (617 serve points) and 9.4 for the women; aces after 1.9 and 2.1; break points saved after 67 and 59 (442 and 483 break points faced); tiebreaks won after about 970 and 1,995 matches, which no career reaches.

ObservedOver all surfaces the curve misses the measured points by at most 0.10, the most for games and sets won, whose k creeps up with n: the players who play many matches are the ones who win, so they are more alike. On a fixed population (players with at least 30 matches in the season, for every n) k is 38 matches for the men's games won against 33, and 9.7 against 8.3 for serve points won. That is why the section's verdict reads the curve at a typical season among the players who play one.

3. How much of the tour average to mix in

The shrinkage constant K is the chance variation of one unit over the true spread between players. Both are measured on player-seasons (three seasons on grass): the true spread is the spread of season rates less their chance part, and the chance part is measured from how much a player's matches vary within the season. That includes the points' own luck and the changing opponents, conditions and form. A binomial model (a beta-binomial's constant) counts the points' luck alone. The constants for Stage 4 are estimated on 2010–2018 only (the adjusted rates on 2013–2018, after the ratings' warm-up), so Stage 4 can use them without look-ahead.

ObservedFor serve points won K is 466 serve points for the men (6.3 matches) and 582 for the women; binomial chance alone would give 200 and 245: a match's serve rate varies 1.9 and 1.8 times as much as point luck explains. Break points behave almost like pure chance within a player (variation 1.1 and 1.1 times binomial). ObservedWith every year (2010–2026) the constants move little: the ratio to the 2010–2018 constant has a median of 1.03 over all surfaces, from 0.81 to 1.14 (tiebreaks aside), and 143 of 144 constants fall inside the 2010–2018 bootstrap range.

5. The next match from a player's history

For every evaluation match from 2019 (the women's box-score rates are evaluated from 2019 on, as the Brief sets; the scores here are 2019–2026), the player's rate in that match is forecast from their earlier matches at every level from 2010, under the time rule. Three forms, each shrunk toward the tour's average on the match's surface with the 2010–2018 constant: career to date; the last 52 weeks; and decayed, every earlier match weighted by half per half-life (chosen per statistic on the men's 2013–2018 matches from 13, 26, 52, 104 and 208 weeks). Each past match is measured against its own surface's average. For serve, return and total points won, the serve and return ratings also forecast them, knowing the opponent. The score is the log loss of the next match's points (or games, sets...) against the tour's average, and the share of the next match's variation beyond point luck that the forecast captures. The best forecast of a statistic is not necessarily the best predictor of who wins; Stage 4 decides that.

ObservedServe points won: the player's history captures 15% (men) and 15% (women) of what is not point luck in the next match; the ratings, which also know the returner, 35% and 34% (2.65 and 2.43 milli-log-loss per serve point better than the average, against 1.13 and 1.10). The three forms are close for most rates; recency helps the serve style (the chosen half-lives are 26 weeks for aces, 26 for double faults, 13 for first serves in) and not skill (serve points won 208 weeks, the longest tried, which is nearly the whole career).

6. Serve and return ratings: adjusting for the opponent

A match's serve rate depends on who returns. Subtracting the opponents' average rates does not fix that, because those averages depend in turn on whom the opponents played. The ratings instead explain every match's serve points at once: the chance that the server wins a point is 1 / (1 + e−(μ + serve − return)), with μ the tour's average on the surface. After a match each rating moves by the points won above expectation, times a step that is large while the rating is uncertain and small once it is well known. Time away widens the uncertainty again. It is Elo on serve points with a step that adapts, as Glickman's Glicko does for wins. A rating for a match holds only earlier events and the same event's earlier rounds (Stage 1's rule), so Stage 4 can use the pre-match values, which are saved for every match.

ObservedFour constants were tuned on the serve points of 2013–2018 (2010–2012 warm up) and frozen: a newcomer's serve rating starts 0.20 below their return rating for the men and 0.10 for the women (newcomers serve worse against the pool they join), with variances of 0.02 and 0.005 (serve, return) on both tours, which widen by 0.0002 (men) and 0.0001 (women) for each week away. All lie inside the grid. On 2019–2026, out of sample, the ratings forecast serve points 2.65 (men) and 2.44 (women) milli-log-loss per point better than the surface's average (3.08 and 2.32 in the tuning years). Among established players a serve rating's spread is 0.17 and a return rating's 0.14 on the log-odds scale (men); serve and return correlate at 0.43.

InferredAdjusted for the opponent, serve and return points won are more reliable, need fewer matches, carry over better between seasons and across surfaces (the table). The opponent mix, not the player, was a large part of the raw rates' noise.

How Stage 3 was built and checked

  • Data. Stage 0's player rows. Measurements: completed matches in the standard format at the evaluation levels (352,340 men's and 141,202 women's player-matches, 2010–2026). Forecast history: every completed singles match from 2010 at every level (891,686 and 771,754 player-matches). A rate counts for a match where both its count and its denominator are written. One Tennis My Life box score (Prague 2026) has an unreadable break-point count, as Stage 2 reported; its hold, break and break-points-faced rates are left out.
  • "All surfaces" measures each match against the tour's 2010–2018 average on its surface, so a player's mix of surfaces does not count as a trait.
  • The time rule. The ratings and every forecast use only earlier events and earlier rounds of the same event. The tests check the rating pass against a plain match-by-match pass, a hand-made history and a fresh pass over only what the rule lets each match see, and the look-back sums (career, last 52 weeks, decayed) against a row-by-row reading of the rule. A probe on the real history redrew the serve points of every match a cut may not see: 0 of 3,300,122 protected pre-match values moved, while 185,839 later ones did.
  • Known on simulated players. The split-half correlation follows the Spearman–Brown curve and recovers k; the shrinkage constant is recovered, and the binomial-only constant is shown too small when matches vary beyond point luck; the carry-over ceiling gives about 1 for a rate that never changes; the ratings recover true serve and return strengths.

Terms used in this section

Row
A candidate in one form (career, last 52 weeks, decayed, this surface, opponent-adjusted; or the one form a single feature has), for one tour.
Walk-forward
For each season from 2013 (the women's box-score rows from 2019): fitted on the evaluation-level matches of 2010 to the season before, scored on that season. A season whose training set holds fewer than 300 matches, or fewer than 300 on which the feature is not zero, is not scored.
Improvement
The baseline's log loss minus the row's model's, per match, in milli-log-loss (thousandths; a coin scores 693): positive is better. Alone: against a coin. Given Elo: surface Elo plus the feature against surface Elo alone, refitted on the same matches. Given the market: the same with the closing odds, on the main-tour matches that have them. The 95% range comes from the per-match differences.
Null band
The range of the improvements of 20 copies of the feature shuffled within each season. Every match carries 20 random keys shared by every row, so the copies keep the rows' links to one another.
Family-wide line
Per tour and score, each row's improvement is put on a common scale (minus its copies' mean, over their standard deviation). Each copy's largest value over all rows is the best that useless features as correlated as these reach; the line is the largest of the 20. Given Elo it is 9.9 (men) and 8.1 (women) standard deviations.
Reading
Fixed in the plan. Finding: clears the line given Elo, and its improvement given Elo is positive in at least three eras. Possible: above its own band, not the line. No sign: inside its band. Worse: below it. A row whose feature never varies carries no information; a row with no season that passes the training minimum is not scored.
Coverage
The share of the tour's evaluation matches of the row's scored seasons on which it was scored (it exists for both players and its season was fitted).
Directional
A group of fewer than 300 matches: its result shows a direction only.

Why the ranking looks worse than it is

ObservedAdded to surface Elo, the ranking, ranking points and the level played over the last 52 weeks score worse than Elo alone over all their seasons. Season by season (the chart), the loss sits in the early seasons and fades: the men's ranking scores −2.60 in 2013 and 0.51 in 2026; the women's 0.93 in 2013, below zero in all of the seasons 2014–2022 and 3.15 in 2026. The walk-forward fit for 2013, trained on 2010–2012, weights the men's ranking 0.61 per standard deviation and Elo's log-odds 0.40, against 0.73 for Elo alone; the fit for 2026 weights them 0.24 and 0.72, against 0.89.

InferredThe walk-forward fits begin on 2010–2012, the seasons in which Elo is still warming up from 1,500 for every player. There a ranking corrects an immature rating, so the first fits weight it heavily and Elo lightly; each later fit still carries those seasons, so the weights change only slowly. The plan fixes this window, so the full-period ranking understates every feature that stands in for strength; their 2022–2026 values are the fairer reading.

How Stage 4 was built and checked

  • Data. 182,440 men's and 72,683 women's evaluation-level matches of 2010–2026 (completed or retired); 155,130 and 60,321 of them are evaluation matches (2013–2026). Features read every singles match from 2010 as history.
  • One chronological pass. Every history goes through the study's as-of helper and Stage 3's look-back sums, never a per-row look-back. Stage 3's constants, ratings and Stage 1's Elo are used as they are.
  • The plan reproduced. For 324 of 325 rows, the matches on which a feature exists equal the plan's own coverage counts; the one difference is the men's home country, 57 matches fewer: the 62 matches of the Kingston events of 2026, whose country the name cannot place, less those already without a player's country.
  • Constants set in Stage 4 (before any score; stage4_constants.csv), by Stage 3's method on 2010–2018: match wins 29 matches (men) and 28 (women); break points above expectation: no spread between the men's seasons (every man gets the average), so that statistic carries nothing for the men by construction.
  • The tournament's country. A hand-made table (stage4_event_country.csv) from the city in each event's name. It agrees with tennis-data.co.uk's location on 63,887 of 63,941 linked matches, with the country code of the WTA's 2013–2015 tournament ids on 187 of 187 events, and with the WTA calendar on 644 of 647; every disagreement is listed in stage4_event_country_check.csv.
  • Tests (test/predictability_diagnostic/test_stage4.py): every feature family on hand-made histories against a row-by-row reading of the time rule; the Newton fits against scikit-learn's; walk-forward never seeing the season it scores; the copies staying inside their season; the family-wide line.
