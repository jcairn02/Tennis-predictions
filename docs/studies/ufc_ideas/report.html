Summary

What we tried, and what it did

The predictability diagnostic tested 59 candidate features (325 versions) and found that nothing beats the bookmakers' closing prices, while serve-and-return strength ratings beat Elo most. This second pass borrows the UFC pipeline's tools: a Bradley–Terry rating in place of Elo, ratings solved from closing odds, opponent-adjusted statistics, strength of schedule and fatigue. It adds 99 features (324 versions) on the same 155,130 men's and 60,321 women's matches of 2013–2026, scored with the same walk-forward test, so every number below sits on one scale.

observedBradley–Terry does not replace Elo in tennis

Tuned on the same seasons as Elo, the Bradley–Terry rating is worse than surface Elo by 1.60 (men) and 4.45 (women) milli-log-loss over 2013–2026, and sits further from the bookmakers than Elo does: it closes -8% and -32% of the Elo-to-market gap. A rating solved from closing odds (MESR) adds -0.03 and -0.02 to the odds themselves.

observedOpponent-adjusted statistics beat Elo like the ratings do, not the bookmakers

Men (ATP): Everything + MESR (together) closes 54% of the gap between Elo and the bookmakers (the earlier best, the strength ratings, 28%). Women (WTA): Everything + MESR (together) closes 60% of the gap between Elo and the bookmakers (the earlier best, the strength ratings, 29%). Everything together adds +9.35 (men) and +9.86 (women) to Elo but only +0.31 and +0.32 to the odds. Of the new versions, 10 (men) and 10 (women) are findings given Elo, 35 and 35 of them clear; 16 (men) and 8 (women) clear the family-wide line against the bookmakers, none by a milli-log-loss.

observedOne lead against the odds: the men's individual-statistic profile

The profile of twelve individual statistics (2-week adjperf: aces, double faults, first serves in, first- and second-serve points won, holds, break points, breaks, returns), nearly useless alone, adds +0.51 milli-log-loss to the closing odds for the men (-0.05 to +1.07; +0.92 in 2022–2026), the largest gain on the odds in either study, and +0.02 for the women. The same statistics over 52 weeks, and the long-memory IOS traits, add nothing to the odds.

What we suggest next

  • inferred What the closing line misses, if anything, is recent form in individual statistics, not a stable trait. The gain against the odds sits in the two-week adjperf profile; the 52-week version and the long-memory IOS ratings, the most trait-like measures here, give nothing. The market prices what a player is and may miss a little of how they have played, statistic by statistic, in the last fortnight.
  • suggestion Tighten the men's lead before building on it. A ridge-penalised fit instead of the plain logistic regression, Pinnacle's line instead of the average line, the gain by level and by season, and which statistics carry it. Half a milli-log-loss is about half a percent of the way from a coin flip to the odds: a lead, not an edge.
  • suggestion Then build the same recent-form profile from the point-by-point data. The box score holds twelve statistics; the point logs collected in the other studies hold the finer ones (serve direction, rally length, pressure points), which is where an orthogonal profile would grow.
  • suggestion Keep surface Elo as the base rating. Bradley–Terry, MESR, strength of schedule and the retrospective credit do not improve on it in tennis; the matches are too many and too frequent for a batch rating with one memory to beat a per-player K. The credit for opponents lost to and the women's quality of wins are small findings given Elo only.
  • suggestion Close fatigue as a pre-match family. The late-match decline is not a trait in these data; what fatigue there is shows within a match, not before it.

Decisions for you

  • Whether to pursue the men's individual-statistic profile, and with which tightening first.
  • Which inputs to carry forward as the pre-match baseline: our suggestion is surface Elo with the ratings through the iid model and the two-week adjperf profile, the only inputs that add to the odds at all.
  • The stages' open technical choices, listed in summary.md: the widened tuning grids of Stages A and B, the diagnostic IOS rows added after the first rundown, the tournament-date "week" that lets a few mid-week events see each other, and the data problems found (split identities in 2026, one impossible box score, non-Monday dates).

A Bradley–Terry rating and a market rating

The owner's UFC models rate fighters with a tuned Bradley–Terry rating (BT): at every week, every earlier result is re-solved at once, older results weigh less (a half-life), and a ridge pulls each rating toward zero, or a newcomer toward a lower anchor. A win is credited at what the opponent turned out to be worth, where Elo credits it at what the opponent was worth that day. Here BT is built from wins and losses alone, with the same time rule as Stage 1's Elo, tuned on 2013–2018 and frozen. The market-embedded skill rating (MESR) is the same idea solved from closing odds instead of results. Every forecast is scored by log loss (a coin scores 0.693; lower is better; one milli-log-loss is 0.001).

Terms used in this section

z
A match's statistic on the log-odds scale minus the opponent's norm, over the opponent's typical match-to-match spread (MAD), clipped to ±4.
MAD
Mean absolute deviation: the average distance of the opponent's matches from their own weighted mean, shrunk toward the population's.
adjperf / adjdef
The player's average z of what they produced (adjperf), or of how far they held each opponent below that opponent's own norm (adjdef). In tennis adjdef on serve points won is exactly adjperf on return points won seen from the other side (likewise holds and breaks, and the other mirrored pairs), so those are scored once.
IOS, κ
Implied offence and defence: per statistic, every match's log-odds rate ≈ the server's offence minus the returner's defence, solved jointly over all earlier weeks with recent matches weighted more; κ is the ridge, in typical matches, that pulls a rating toward the average.
Forms
Last 52 weeks (no decay), decayed (a half-life h3), career; all shrunk toward 0 with K3 matches.
Strong, weak
An opponent whose pre-match surface Elo is above (strong) or at or below (weak) the median of main-tour players in 2010–2018.

Terms used in this section

Strength of schedule
The UFC project's measure of the opposition: the mean pre-match strength of the opponents a player met in the last 52 weeks (surface Elo, or the Stage 3 serve and return ratings). "Quality of wins" is the same over the opponents beaten.
Phantom prior
K imaginary opponents of the tour's typical strength mixed into the mean, so that a player with few matches sits near typical: (sum + K × typical) / (n + K). K was chosen on 2013–2018 and frozen.
Revaluation (credit)
For each opponent of the last 52 weeks: their Elo now (before the week of the match being predicted) minus their Elo when they were met. "Later only" also removes the meeting's own Elo change, as the UFC rule does, so that only the opponent's later matches revise the credit.
Late decline
From the Grand Slam point-by-point files: a player's serve points won in the third set onward minus in the first two, against the opponent's same change. Positive means holding up better late.
Grinder
Points won above what the Stage 3 ratings expected, in long matches (minutes per set above the tour's upper quartile) minus in short ones.
Reliability
The correlation of a player's measure between two random halves of their matches: near 0, the measure is luck; it must also beat the same measure on pure-luck replays, which have no fatigue.
Given Elo, given the market
Milli-log-loss better (positive) than surface Elo, or the closing odds, alone; walk-forward by season, beside 20 shuffled copies. The family-wide line given Elo is 3.69 (men) and 6.80 (women) standard deviations of the copies.

What we found

Can information known before a tennis match predict the winner better than an Elo rating, and better than the bookmakers' closing prices? We tested 59 candidate features (325 versions in all) on 155,130 men's and 60,321 women's professional matches from 2013 to 2026, always predicting each season from earlier seasons only.

observedThe bookmakers stay ahead

No feature improves on their closing prices by more than 0.3% of the way from a coin flip to their prices: far too little to bet on.

observedA few things beat Elo, modestly

Serve-and-return strength ratings do best: they close about 28% (men) and 29% (women) of the gap between Elo and the bookmakers. Recent form, recent workload and a new surface add less.

inferredPre-match records look priced in

If there is an edge, it is more likely in live (in-play) data, or in markets less sharp than the bookmakers' closing prices.

What we suggest next

  • suggestion Test the best features together. One model with the strength ratings, recent points won and recent workload, checked against the bookmakers. The repaired feature comparison supersedes the original results. Failure of these features would not close the pre-match research route.
  • suggestion Try live (in-play) data. Point observations may help update day-level performance estimates. Testing this needs time-stamped point data and live prices, which this study did not have.
  • suggestion Test against the market you would actually bet on. Polymarket's tennis prices may be less sharp than the bookmakers' closing prices.
  • suggestion Fix the known data problems first. Some players appear under two ids in 2026 and some lower-level scores are misread; the effects here are small, but any rebuild inherits them.

Decisions for you

  • Which features to carry forward. Our suggestion: the strength ratings, recent points won and recent workload.
  • What to do next: the combined test, live data, or the Polymarket comparison.
  • The open technical choices from the earlier stages, listed in summary.md. Stage 2's estimate of luck, quoted above, is one of them.
