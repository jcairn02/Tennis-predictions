Ten Challenger careers, checked match by match

We drew ten players at random from the ATP Challenger Tour, from the top 100 down to about No. 700. For each one we listed every professional singles match of their career from the ATP's official website, then looked for every one of those matches in the tennis data we downloaded. This page shows how much of each career we hold, what is missing and why, and how much detail we have about the matches we do hold.

In short: 99.1% of the matches on these players' official record are somewhere in our data (98% with at least the final score), and nearly all of their Challenger and tour-level matches come with serve and return statistics (98%). The thin part is the bottom of the pyramid. ITF main-draw matches make up 50% of these careers, and before 2025 only 0.1% of them have more than the score. Below the Challenger main draw, no source has results after early June 2026. Deeper detail, point by point or shot by shot, exists for 2% of matches. And one kind of professional match is missing from both the official ATP record and our data: qualifying at ITF events.

Question A

Who we checked, and how they were picked

A player was eligible if they were ranked between 1 and 700 on the ATP ranking of 31 August 2026 (the newest ranking in our download), had played at least five Challenger main-draw matches in the year before, and were born in 1994 or later. The age limit keeps each whole career inside our download, which starts in 2010. We split the eligible players into five ranking bands and drew two from each band at random, so the sample runs from players on the edge of the top 100 down to players around No. 700.

Question B

Every match, lined up against the official record

The ATP's website lists every match a player has played at events that count for the ATP ranking: ITF World Tennis Tour events (the old "Futures", the lowest professional level), Challengers, and tour-level events such as ATP 250s and Grand Slams, with their qualifying rounds, plus Davis Cup. That is the official record we checked against. It covers each player's whole career, from their first professional match (2012 at the earliest) to 21 September 2026. The one kind of professional match it leaves out, qualifying at ITF events, is covered separately under C.

In total the ATP record lists 4,025 matches for the ten players, from 146 (Nicolas Villalon) to 748 (Jan Choinski). As a check on our transcription we compared each player's tour-level win–loss record in it with the headline figure on their ATP profile. They agree for nine of ten players. The difference for Adam Walton (25–48 on the profile, 25–46 transcribed) is exactly the tour-level matches in the weeks of the record the reading tool could not see (January 2024, see How we checked). Question C

What share is in our data, and what is missing

99.1% of the 4,025 matches are in at least one of our sources, and 37 are not. The share hardly varies by player, from 94% (Nicolas Villalon) to 100% (Adam Walton); what decides it is the level and the date. Of the 37 missing matches, 5 are lower-level matches played after early June 2026, when our only source for those levels stops; 11 single matches are missing from tournaments we otherwise hold; 21 belong to tournaments absent from every source.         The blind spot in both records: ITF qualifying

        The ATP record leaves out qualifying rounds at ITF events, and so does every source in our download, so these matches are not in the totals above. We could read the ITF's own record only for Samuele Pieri's 2023 season before its website began refusing automated requests. That season alone has 30 ITF qualifying matches, next to 42 matches on the ATP record for the same season. None of them are in our data with a result, and none appears even as a schedule entry. For a player who still enters ITF qualifying, the whole career is noticeably longer than the ATP record shows, and that extra part is invisible to us.

        The other direction: rows in our data that match nothing official

        We also checked the opposite direction: rows in our data for these players that match no official match. Our sources hold 7,847 rows for the ten players (each source counted separately), and 99.1% of them match an official match. Of the 33 rows with a result that do not:

        • 18 come from Walton's January 2024, the weeks of the official record we could not read.
        • 2 are from tournaments still being played this week, not yet posted on the official record.
        • 10 are ITF results the Sackmann archive credits to Gauthier Onclin in 2018–2020 that the official record does not have. In 4 of those weeks the official record places the player at a different tournament, so at least those results belong to someone else.
        • 3 repeat, under another tournament name and date, matches the same source already holds (Jan Choinski).

        Some rows that do match an official match disagree with it on a detail. These still count as present in the figures above:

        • 36 Tennis My Life rows are filed under the wrong week, some by as much as ten months.
        • 2 Sackmann rows name a different opponent from the official record (identical scores, so the same match).
        • 80 of Nicolas Villalon's matches sit under a second player record in the Sackmann archive.

        Question D

        How much detail we hold about each match

        A match can sit in our data at very different depths. We graded every match by the richest layer any of our sources holds for it:

          The pattern is set by level and date more than by the player. For Challenger and tour-level matches, 98% come with serve and return statistics. ITF main-draw matches are the opposite: before 2025 only 0.1% have them, because our sources kept only the score at that level. For January 2025 to June 2026 the Sackmann archive has statistics for 92% of ITF main-draw matches. So the early part of a career, usually spent at ITF level, is known by results only, and the Challenger part has the standard scoreboard totals. Across the ten careers the share with serve statistics runs from 49% (Daniil Glinka, 59% of whose career is ITF matches) to 77% (Adam Walton, 23%).

            Deeper layers are rare at this level: 85 of 4,025 matches (2%) have a point-by-point or shot-by-shot record.             Method

            How we checked

            1. The sample. Drawn by src/eda/career_coverage/select_players.py from the Tennis My Life ranking of 31 August 2026 and its Challenger results (seed 20260923). Two further players per band were drawn as reserves in case a pick's career started before 2010; none was needed.
            2. The official record. For each player and season we read the ATP website's player-activity data, and the ITF website's activity data for ITF events, on 23 September 2026. We transcribed every tournament and match line by line into text files kept with the study, and checked each season's match count against the count the site gives for each tournament. The ITF website began refusing automated requests while it was being read, so its record covers only Samuele Pieri's 2023 season; we did not try to get around the block. The headline figures therefore use the ATP record, and ITF qualifying is reported separately.
            3. Finding each match in our data. We searched all eight sources in the download (listed below) for each player. A row counts as the same match when it is the same player, the opponent agrees, and the date falls in the same tournament week. Opponents are matched by the ATP player code where a source has it, and otherwise by name, allowing for accents, hyphens and middle names. A row that could fit two official matches equally well is counted as unmatched rather than guessed.
            4. Grading detail. Each official match takes the richest layer any source holds for it (the ladder in section D). "Serve and return statistics" means both players' serve totals are present, not just the score.

            Caveats

            • The official record is our transcription of the ATP and ITF websites through a web-reading tool. Match counts were checked per tournament, and every match our data holds was matched back to it, so an omission would show up as an unmatched row in our data. A second, shorter read of every season listed only its tournaments, and every tournament missing from the first transcription was fetched again.
            • Matches outside professional events are not counted: junior events, US college (NCAA) matches, exhibitions and doubles. Several of these players played college tennis, so the official record has gaps of whole seasons that are not gaps in our data.
            • Walkovers are counted as matches because the official record lists them; there is nothing to measure in them, so none can have statistics.
            • Ten players is a small sample. It shows the pattern for this kind of player, not exact rates for the whole Challenger tour.
            • "Serve and return statistics" are the totals a scoreboard keeps (aces, double faults, serve points, first serves in, points won on first and second serve, service games, break points). They say nothing about rally length, shot types or point order unless a deeper layer exists.
