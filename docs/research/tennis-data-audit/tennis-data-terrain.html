Tennis Data Terrain

Tennis has no league table and no kickoff weekend. It is a year-round circuit of some sixteen hundred professional tournaments, run by three organisations, on which a player can be in a $50,000 event in a Serbian spa town one week and a Grand Slam the next. This page maps that circuit against the two things the project needs to know before choosing where to start: where the betting money actually sits, and where usable data is thick or thin.

01

Who runs what

Three organisations, four privately owned tournaments, and one shared currency: ranking points. The data rights sit with different partners for each, which is why no single vendor covers the whole sport.

The ITF is the world federation: it writes the rules, runs the national-team competitions and the Olympic event, and operates the entry-level professional circuit. The ATP (men) and WTA (women) are separate player-and-tournament associations that run the weekly tours. The four Grand Slams belong to national federations and sit above all of it; they are the only events where both tours, juniors, doubles and wheelchair play the same fortnight on the same grounds.

What ties the whole structure together is that every level awards points on the same ranking list, so the same player identity runs from an ITF qualifying round to a Slam final. That is the good news for a model. The bad news is that the official data for each layer is licensed to a different partner.

02

The ladder

Seven rungs, one ranking. The top five rungs hold about one match in ten; the bottom two hold nine in ten, and one of those two takes a fifth of the money while the other takes almost none.

Each rung is named by the ranking points its winner receives: a Slam pays 2000, a Masters 1000 pays 1000, and so on down to an ITF M15 event whose champion earns a handful. The number of tournaments grows three- to five-fold each time you step down, and so does the number of matches. Attention by rung: what is actually published

Audience numbers are as top-heavy as the money, and so is their availability. The four Slams and the two tour finals publish audience figures every year; below the 1000s, nobody publishes anything, which is itself the finding. 04

Where data is thick, and where it is thin

Seven kinds of data a match model might want, across the rungs of the ladder. Colour is evidence strength in this repository's audit, not a quality score.

"Data" in tennis is not one thing. A result row (who won, set scores) exists somewhere for most professional matches of this decade, but no single inspected file holds them all, and the freshest public archive stops in mid-2026. Serve-and-return statistics exist for the main tours since 1991 but only reached the ITF level in 2025. Bookmaker odds exist as one row per match, of unstated vintage, for the main tours only; the register is explicit that these are not timestamped, executable prices. Point-by-point tapes exist as samples and paid products. Polymarket listings exist everywhere, but the money does not. Which sources reach which rung

The register's shortlist, laid against the ladder. A filled dot means the audit inspected an actual payload for that rung; a half dot means the provider documents coverage that nobody here has opened; a dashed ring means the source does not reach that rung at all.

"Credible" in the register is not a score. It is the distinction between a payload someone downloaded and measured, and a coverage claim on a vendor page. The count that matters for choosing a starting point is the first kind: how many independently measured sources can be cross-checked against each other at a given rung. Note the families that overlap: Open Tennis Data reuses the Sackmann lineage among others, and TennisData.app's lineage is unverified, so three dots in one column are not always three witnesses.

07

The season

Eleven months, four surfaces blocks, four peaks. Where a tier sits in the year decides when its data arrives and, in this window, how big the market was when it played.

The calendar is the same every year: hard courts in Australia and the Americas until March, clay in Europe through Roland-Garros in early June, a five-week grass season ending at Wimbledon, North American hard courts to the US Open in September, then Asia and European indoor events until the tour finals in November. The bottom strip shows how many tennis events Polymarket listed in each month of the census window, and how much money they attracted. The ramp is the platform's, not the sport's.

08

Questions to sit with

This page does not pick a starting point. It does try to make the trade-offs visible enough that the choice is yours to make on evidence. These are the questions the figures above keep raising.

  1. Where is data thick and the market thick?Sections 3 and 4 overlap on exactly one band: the main tours from the 250s up, where seven to nine measured sources, decades of statistics, one row of bookmaker odds per match and 64% of Polymarket's money coincide. Everything below has either the money without the odds baseline (Challengers) or the listings without the money (ITF).
  2. What would a Challenger model be judged against?One dollar in five sits on Challengers and WTA 125s, yet no file in the register carries measured bookmaker prices below the 250s, and the integrity agency's alerts are migrating to that tier. A model there has no independent price to beat and a different risk profile to price in. Is that an opportunity, a warning, or both?
  3. Is a tier the right unit at all?Sackmann's level "A" merges 500s and 250s; Polymarket's "ATP" series merges Challengers and qualifying; a Slam's first round is a very different match from its final. Sections 2 and 3 suggest that draw stage and player rank may separate populations more cleanly than tournament tier does.
  4. Does the model need to know the tour, or only see it?Section 6: one schema, same contract types, same surfaces, but a fifth set at men's Slams, a second tier four times larger, and roughly twice the money on the men's side. Six in seven main-tour men also played a Challenger main draw in 2025, and no player appears in both tours. Which of those differences must be designed in, and which can be left to a feature such as best-of or tour flag?
  5. How much history does the target actually need?Main-tour statistics go back to 1991; Challenger to 2008; ITF to 2025. If a season of statistics is enough, the lower tiers are open. If a decade is needed, only the main tours qualify, and the WTA lower-tier file's 43% row drop between 2024 and 2025 is a reminder that even recent history shifts under you.
  6. Which comparisons survive the platform ramp?Polymarket's monthly listings grew from a few hundred to several thousand across the window, and March's volume fields are largely missing. Any tier that played early looks smaller than it is. Shares within a summer month are safe; whole-window rankings are not, and the Australian Open is the clearest casualty.
  7. Do outrights belong in the same scope as matches?Four percent of the money, on twenty-six tournament-winner markets that traded for months before the first ball, with the three largest single markets in the census being futures rather than matches. They need a draw-and-bracket model, not a match model. Is that a second product or a distraction?
  8. Which rung does the register let you start on tomorrow?Section 5 counts measured sources: nine at the Slams, seven at the 1000s and 500/250s, three at the Challengers, two at ITF. The half dots are not absences, they are files nobody has opened yet. Choosing a rung also chooses how much of the audit is still ahead of you.

Sources and method

Where every number came from, what was inferred, and how to reproduce the measured parts.

Measured in this repository (22 Sep 2026)

  • Polymarket volume by tier. Events from data/studies/tennis_data_audit/polymarket/events.jsonl (Gamma snapshot 10 Sep 2026, sporting-date window 10 Sep 2025 to 10 Sep 2026, nine football records removed; 33,320 events, 406,190 contracts). Volume is the event's volume field, summed by tier. 1,837 events have no volume field and contribute zero; the census report explains why missing is not zero. Script: src/eda/tennis_data_audit/measure_tier_terrain.py, output data/studies/tennis_data_audit/tier_terrain/tier_measurements.json. Run with an explicitly named interpreter (the project's tennis environment does not exist on this machine).
  • Tier assignment rule (inferred, then cross-checked). Slams, tour finals and every named Masters 1000, WTA 1000, ATP/WTA 500 and 250 event of the 2025–26 calendars are matched by their Polymarket league name through an alias list (Cincinnati Open, Internazionali BNL d'Italia, Mubadala Citi DC Open, and so on); the tour comes from the product series or slug prefix, so a shared name such as "China Open" is a WTA 1000 on one side and an ATP 500 on the other. Names containing "Qualification" go to a qualifying bucket, "Juniors" to juniors. Polymarket's two Australian Open leagues also contain qualifying (records dated before the 18 January main draw) and junior matches (nine-contract events from 24 January, girls' matches included under the men's label); those are moved to the qualifying and juniors buckets, which is why the Slam bucket holds 1,005 main-draw records rather than the 1,356 the raw labels give. Any remaining ATP- or WTA-series event with only a place name is assumed to be Challenger or WTA 125 level. Cross-check of that bucket against tournament names in the Sackmann 2025–26 files: 47% of its volume ($303M) carries a name the archive knows only as a Challenger or WTA 125 (Busan CH, Augsburg CH, Abidjan 2 CH, Kigali 2 CH, Birmingham 125, Austin 125, Paris 125, Madrid 125); 39% ($250M) carries a town name that hosts events at more than one level (Antalya, Astana, Bastad, Bogota); 6% ($39M) carries names the archive knows only at ITF level (Roehampton, Kingston, Sion, Quebec City), all July–September 2026 events after the archive's June cutoff, trading at Challenger-scale volumes; 8% ($54M) matched nothing (Vancouver, Philadelphia, Saint-Malo, Plovdiv). Numbered town names (Oeiras 3, Kigali 2: 979 records, $89M) are kept in this bucket because every ITF listing on Polymarket sits in a separate ITF product with itf- slugs, and their median volume is $61K against $1.9K for ITF listings. Eight sponsor-named events whose 2026 tier could not be confirmed (Estoril Open, Athens Open, The Memphis Classic, Ostrava Open, Merida Open Akron, Palermo, Upper Austria Ladies Linz, European Open) carry $66M, or 2.1% of volume, inside the 500 and 250 buckets. Series-less legacy records use the title prefix before the colon.
  • Schema and player overlap. All five 2025 Sackmann match files have the same 49 columns with identical headers (checked 22 Sep 2026). Player overlap counts a player once per file by Sackmann ID within a tour (ATP: levels G, M, A, F versus level C versus Futures; WTA: G, PM, P, I, F versus level C versus ITF levels) and compares names across tours.
  • Match counts, statistics depth, rankings by tier. 2025 files of the Sackmann archival mirror (PUB003) in data/studies/tennis_data_audit/public/raw/: atp_matches_2025.csv, atp_matches_qual_chall_2025.csv, atp_matches_futures_2025.csv, wta_matches_2025.csv, wta_matches_qual_itf_2025.csv. A row "has statistics" when w_svpt is present; the 2024 comparison figures are from the register's public-data audit.
  • Bookmaker odds coverage. Local tennis-data.co.uk workbooks data/raw/tennis_data_couk/{atp,wta}/{2025,2026}.xlsx, grouped by the Series (ATP) or Tier (WTA) column; a row has an odds value when B365W, PSW or AvgW is non-empty. The 2026 files stop at 6–7 June 2026 and are the inherited snapshots the register describes as stale.
  • Polymarket price history. Three sampled moneylines from the register's market-history audit (Korea Open Sept 2025, Wimbledon and US Open 2026); raw responses in data/studies/tennis_data_audit/market_history/.
  • Source × rung matrix. Dots are transcribed from the source register entries and the three audit reports; filled where the register records an inspected payload for that population, half where it records provider documentation only.

Known weaknesses of this page

  • Tier assignment is name-based inference; expect a few errors at the 250-versus-Challenger boundary and inside the WTA 125-versus-ITF boundary. The ITF, Slam and 1000 rows are robust.
  • Lifetime volume is a platform-reported field; it is not a count of fills in the window, it may double-count maker and taker sides, and it is missing on nearly half of the March 2026 records.
  • Audience figures mix reach, unique viewers and single-broadcast averages from different countries; they are illustrative of order of magnitude, not comparable across events.
  • Match counts are physical rows in one archive's 2025 files, not audited tour completeness; the register documents duplicate keys, malformed rows and level-label oddities in the same files.
